# A real hand-edit (before / after)

Unlike the synthetic pairs, this is a real one: an actual opening, first as a competent-but-generic
draft, then as the author rewrote it by hand. Both are clean — neither has a banned phrase. The
difference is the thing no scanner can see (see `guide/03-stance-vs-cadence.md`).

**Before — competent, generic, no one home**
> A company cuts forty people and the headline writes itself: the machines came for the jobs.
> There's a tidy version of this, the one where AI finally lets you do more with less, and every
> CFO who reads it hears permission to swing the axe. I've watched enough of these announcements
> to know the press release is the least interesting document in the building.

**After — the hand-edit**
> I spent a couple of hours last month walking a room full of executives through the AI systems
> we'd built. The numbers landed. The story didn't. They believed the slides and didn't believe
> what the slides were for, and I've watched that exact thing happen in enough of these rooms to
> recognize it now. It's the same disbelief sitting underneath every layoff announcement this year.

**What changed, and why it matters**

The before opens on a *hypothetical* — a generic "a company cuts forty people" that could headline
any article on the topic. It performs knowingness ("the least interesting document in the building")
without earning it.

The after opens on a *specific thing that happened to the writer*: a real room, a real couple of
hours, a real moment where the numbers landed and the story didn't. Nothing in the before is
*wrong*; it's just anonymous. The edit doesn't add adjectives or drama — it replaces the
hypothetical with the lived scene, which is the one move a subtractive pass can never make for you.
That's the whole argument of the guide in a single pair: cutting slop gets you the clean *before*;
only the writer can supply the *after*.
