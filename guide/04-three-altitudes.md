# Slop lives at three altitudes

Why does a draft pass every checker you own and still read like a machine wrote it?

Because the checker only works at one height. It reads every word and sees nothing above the word, and most of what makes writing feel machine-made is happening higher up. Slop is not one problem. It lives at three altitudes, and each one needs a different tool to catch it.

The token altitude is words and phrases. The structure altitude is the shape of a paragraph and the rhythm underneath it. The stance altitude is the point of view, whether anyone actually decided anything. A fix aimed at one altitude does nothing for the other two, which is exactly why a draft can pass a clean scan and still be dead on the page. You cleared the cheapest layer and never went up.

## Token altitude

This is the lowest and cheapest altitude, the layer of literal strings: the banned openers, the buzzwords, the pile of em-dashes, the "serves as" dodge. The owner is a lexical scanner, the one piece of this a script does well. The operation is search-and-replace. The tell is a specific word or phrase you can point at and match.

Run this pass first because it is free and it never gets tired. A machine flags "it's worth noting that" in a second and asks nothing in return, and there is no reason to ship tokens a script would have caught. Just do not confuse a clean token pass for a clean draft. It clears the cheapest layer and nothing above it.

## Structure altitude

One level up, the words are all fine and the shape is the problem. It shows up as paragraphs written in one register the whole way through, sentences of identical length droning in a row, and the throat-clear-then-deliver rhythm that keeps announcing a point before it lands.

The thing to watch here is the shared dramatic-pause budget, because it is the piece people get wrong most. Three constructions produce the pause-then-deliver beat: the em-dash, the colon-sentence, and the dramatic one-line fragment. They are one beat with three different punctuation marks, so they draw from a single pool, not three separate ones. As a rough working rule, one dramatic pause of any kind per 150 words or so is about where the rhythm starts to perform; the exact number matters far less than the principle, which is that all three kinds count against the same pool.

That shared pool is the whole point, because of the trap it closes. If the three had separate budgets, an over-used em-dash could be swapped for a colon and called fixed. The em-dash count drops, the colon count rises inside its own allowance, and the reader hears the identical beat. Nothing changed but the punctuation. The only real fix is to remove the pause, not relocate it: rewrite into plain connected prose, a conjunction or a subordinate clause or two flat sentences with no theatrical gap. The test is simple. Keep the pause when it is carrying real weight, and cut it when it is only there for drama.

The owner at this altitude is a reader watching shape instead of meaning, or a structural pass that reads for padding. The operation is removing a whole unit, a pause or a redundant sentence or a section that restates the one before it. The tell is rhythm that performs while every individual sentence stays clean, which is exactly what the lexical scanner is blind to.

## Stance altitude

This is the top altitude, and the one no scanner will ever reach. Stance is whether a person took a position and will be wrong about it out loud. A sentence can be spotless at the token altitude, well-shaped at the structure altitude, and still have no one behind it. The stance essay works that failure through in detail; on this map it is enough to note that both lower altitudes can read clean while the sentence stays empty, which is why stance is the altitude a passing scan tells you least about.

The owner here is the writer, or a judge that reads the draft beside real samples and asks whether it came from someone who decided something. The operation is not subtraction, because you cannot delete your way to a stance. You add one, by putting a claim on the table that could turn out wrong. The tell is a paragraph that flatters the reader, promises an insight, and never pays for it.

## Why the altitudes stay separate

A token-clean draft can be structurally bloated. A structurally tight draft can be stance-empty. The three problems do not travel together, and a fix for one does not touch the others. This is where most editing goes wrong. A draft reads generic, so someone reaches for another banned-word rule, which is a token fix for a stance problem. The scanner was already passing, so adding a rule to it changes nothing.

Diagnose the height before you reach for a tool. If a script can point at the problem, it is at the token altitude and a script can fix it. If the words are fine but the shape or rhythm is off, you are at the structure altitude and the fix is to cut, not to reword. And if the draft is clean, tight, and still says nothing, the problem is at the altitude no rule can reach, and only a writer deciding to mean something answers it.
