# Copywriting Methodology

The durable principles of persuasive copy: clarity, specificity, proof, and the mechanics of an offer.
This is a working method, not a style guide. It covers how to make a claim believable, how to keep a
reader moving, and how to close without begging.

Where a principle traces to a named copywriter, it is attributed and paraphrased. The deep,
per-practitioner treatment lives in the companion doc, `masters-as-lenses.md`. Sentence-level cadence
lives in `sentence-rhythm.md`.

---

## The one question underneath everything

Before you analyze a single line, ask the reader's question for them:

> "What's in it for me, and why should I believe you?"

Every principle below is a way of answering that question honestly. Copy fails when it answers a question
the writer cares about (how clever we are, how much we built) instead of the one the reader is actually
asking.

---

## Part 1: Five core principles

These apply to every piece, regardless of format.

### 1. Write like a knowledgeable friend, not a salesperson

Confident, but not superior. You know the subject; you are not lecturing from above. The fastest way to
sound trustworthy is to admit what you don't know and to name the limits of your own claim.

- Salesperson: "Our revolutionary platform transforms operations with cutting-edge automation."
- Knowledgeable friend: "We've run this workflow for six months. Some of it works well. Some we're still
  fixing. Here's what we've learned so far."

The second version lowers the reader's guard because it isn't trying to. Honesty reads as competence.

### 2. Address one person, not "organizations"

A single human reads your copy and makes the decision. Write to that person. Collective nouns
("companies," "teams," "enterprises") put distance between you and the reader and make the writing feel
like a memo.

- Distant: "Organizations are adopting new tools to manage this challenge."
- Direct: "If you're stuck managing this by hand, you're not the only one. Here's what's working."

The exception is when you're genuinely describing group behavior. Don't force "you" where it distorts the
meaning.

### 3. Be specific: numbers and details, not adjectives

A concrete number is believable because it can be checked. A vague claim invites doubt, because there's
nothing to test. "Cut review time from four hours to twenty minutes" beats "save significant time," and
it beats it precisely because a reader could catch you lying.

- Vague: "We significantly improved our process and saved substantial resources."
- Specific: "We cut a weekly report from four hours to about twenty minutes."

Numbers create mental anchors. Details create pictures. Adjectives create nothing. The reader has seen
"significant" a thousand times, and it has stopped meaning anything.

### 4. Cut until every word earns its place

Most first drafts carry twenty to thirty percent padding. The reliable move is to write the draft, then
cut a fifth of the words without losing a single fact. Filler phrases to hunt and delete:

- "It's worth noting that..."
- "This represents..."
- "In today's fast-moving landscape..."
- "What this really means is..."

A useful shape for a paragraph: state the fact, give a brief read on it, land the point, stop. The
temptation is to keep explaining after you've made the point. Resist it.

### 5. Make it scannable

Most readers skim before they commit to reading. Structure earns that first pass:

- Short paragraphs (two to three sentences).
- Bullets for anything that's actually a list.
- Headers that describe the content, not just label it.
- A visual break every few paragraphs.

A wall of text tells the reader the writing will be work. White space tells them it won't be.

## Part 2: The reader's self-interest comes first

The reader is not interested in your product. They are interested in what your product does for them.
This isn't cynicism; it's the starting condition of all copy. Claude Hopkins, the early-twentieth-century
adman who treated advertising as a testable science, put the idea plainly (paraphrasing): people care
nothing for your interests, only for service to themselves.

Two practical consequences follow.

**Lead with the benefit, support with the feature.** A feature is what the thing is. A benefit is what
the reader gets. Never make the reader do the translation.

- Feature only: "Includes automated scheduling."
- Benefit, then feature: "Stop chasing people for meeting times. Scheduling runs itself."

The reliable structure is Feature, then Advantage, then Benefit, and you never skip a step:

- Feature: "It syncs across every calendar you use."
- Advantage: "So there are no double-bookings."
- Benefit: "You stop apologizing for conflicts you didn't cause."

**Frame around the job the reader is trying to do.** A durable framing from jobs-to-be-done thinking:
*"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."* Copy written from the job sounds like
the reader's own thoughts read back to them, which is the highest compliment a line of copy can earn.

- Feature-first: "Our tool uses automation to generate first drafts."
- Job-first: "When a deadline lands on me, I want a usable first draft in minutes, so I can spend my time
  editing instead of staring at a blank page."

## Part 3: One idea per sentence

Complexity is not the same as sophistication. A sentence carrying three ideas asks the reader to hold all
three at once, and most readers won't. Split it. Let each idea land before the next arrives.

- Overloaded: "Our platform, which integrates with the tools you already use and requires no migration,
  reduces manual work while also improving accuracy across every team that touches the process."
- One idea per sentence: "It works with the tools you already use. No migration. Less manual work, and
  fewer mistakes."

This is partly a rhythm decision (the full treatment is in `sentence-rhythm.md`), but it's also a
comprehension decision. Short, single-idea sentences are the load-bearing beams of clear copy. Longer
sentences build momentum; they aren't for cramming.

## Part 4: Proof turns a claim into an argument

Any claim can be asserted. Only a proven claim persuades. Four kinds of proof, roughly in order of
strength:

1. **Self-verifiable proof.** The strongest kind: a claim the reader can test right now, without trusting
   you. "Count the hours you spent on this last week" makes the reader prove your point to themselves.
   Early direct-response writers built whole campaigns on invitations to check a claim on the spot.
2. **Specific numbers.** As in Principle 3, a precise figure with its conditions attached ("measured
   across a year, in one mid-sized team") reads as a record, not a boast.
3. **Social proof.** Evidence that people like the reader got the result. The persuasion researcher
   Robert Cialdini catalogued why this works: we look to similar others to decide what's reasonable.
   Specificity matters here too. "A forty-person team" beats "our customers."
4. **Borrowed authority.** External sources, cited honestly. Useful, but the weakest of the four, because
   the reader knows you chose which studies to quote.

The failure mode to avoid is proof by adverb. "Significantly," "dramatically," "substantially" are claims
wearing the costume of evidence. They prove nothing. If you can't replace the adverb with a number or an
example, you don't yet have the proof. You have a hope.

Honesty is a proof multiplier. Acknowledging a limitation ("this doesn't help teams under a certain size,
because there isn't enough volume to justify the setup") makes every other claim more credible, because it
shows you're describing reality rather than selling a fantasy.

## Part 5: The offer and the call to action

An offer is the exchange you're proposing: this value, for this action, at this cost or risk. A call to
action (CTA) is the specific next step. Both fail in the same predictable ways.

**Make the offer concrete.** "Learn more" is not an offer. "See the twenty-minute version of the report
you currently spend four hours on" is. State what the reader gets, what it takes, and what it costs, in
that order, so the value is in place before the price.

**Reduce the risk of saying yes.** Every CTA asks the reader to spend something, whether time, attention,
money, or the small social cost of being wrong. Lower that cost. A trial, a guarantee, a sample, a "no
card required": each one shrinks the leap. The direct-marketing writer Dan Kennedy's framing
(paraphrased): there are no unreasonable buyers, only offers that ask for too much before proving enough.

**One CTA, said like a human.** Competing calls to action split the reader's attention and lower the odds
they take any of them. Pick the single most important next step. And phrase it conversationally. "Let's
talk" outperforms "Schedule a consultation" because it sounds like a person, not a form.

**Match the message across the journey.** The words in the ad should match the words on the landing page
should match the words in the follow-up. This is the "message match" principle from conversion copywriter
Joanna Wiebe. When the language shifts between steps, the reader feels a small jolt of "wait, is this the
same thing?" Friction is where conversions die.

## Part 6: Structural frameworks

Frameworks are scaffolding, not recipes. They keep you from forgetting a load-bearing part. Reach for the
one that fits the job.

| Framework | Shape | Best for |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| **AIDA** | Attention, Interest, Desire, Action | General-purpose, sales copy |
| **PAS** | Problem, Agitate, Solution | Pain-driven copy, problem-solving products |
| **BAB** | Before, After, Bridge | Transformation and case-study narratives |
| **FAB** | Feature, Advantage, Benefit | Product descriptions, spec-heavy pages |
| **4 Ps** | Promise, Picture, Proof, Push | Landing pages, long-form sales |
| **4 U's** | Unique, Ultra-specific, Urgent, Useful | Scoring headlines |

Two carry most of the weight and are worth expanding.

**PAS (Problem, Agitate, Solution).** Name the pain. Make its cost vivid and specific. Then present the
resolution. The "agitate" step is where honesty matters most, because you're quantifying a real cost, not
inventing fear. "This task eats about four hours a week" agitates by being true; "this is destroying your
business" agitates by lying, and the reader can tell.

**BAB (Before, After, Bridge).** Show the current painful state. Show the desired state. Then position the
path between them. This is the natural shape of a transformation story, and it works because the reader
recognizes themselves in the "before."

For headlines specifically, score every draft on the **4 U's**: is it Unique, Ultra-specific, Urgent, and
Useful? A headline that's specific and useful but generic and un-urgent still beats one that's merely
clever, because the reader is scanning for relevance, not admiring your wordplay.

## Part 7: Named copywriters as lenses (a short index)

When copy isn't working and you can't say why, run it past a few of these lenses. Each is a question in
someone else's voice. Full treatment in `masters-as-lenses.md`; the short version:

- **David Ogilvy.** Have I done the research? Am I respecting the reader's intelligence? Is the headline
  doing most of the work?
- **Claude Hopkins.** Can I make this more specific? Is this claim testable?
- **Gary Halbert.** Does this sound like a person talking? Where's the honest, human part?
- **Eugene Schwartz.** Am I channeling a desire the reader already has, or trying to manufacture one?
- **John Caples.** Would I actually click this? Where's the before-and-after?
- **Gary Provost.** Read it aloud. Does it have rhythm, or does it drone?
- **April Dunford.** What would the reader use if we didn't exist, and why are we the better fit?
- **Ann Handley.** Is the reader the hero of this, or is my product?

Most strong copy blends several. A cold email often wants Halbert's conversational directness plus
Hopkins's specifics. A landing page wants a Caples-grade headline over Ogilvy-grade density with
Hopkins-grade proof. Don't marry one lens.

## Part 8: How to evaluate a draft

You can judge copy without a rubric if you check the load-bearing dimensions honestly:

- **Headline.** Does it stop the right reader and promise something specific? A generic headline caps
  everything below it, because most people never read past it.
- **Specificity.** Real numbers and concrete examples, or a fog of adjectives?
- **Reader-centricity.** Count the "you"s against the "we"s. If the copy talks more about you than about
  the reader, it's inward-facing.
- **Clarity.** Simple words, short sentences, low jargon. Could a smart outsider follow it?
- **Differentiation.** Does it say something a competitor couldn't paste their name into? If it's
  swappable, it isn't positioning.
- **Proof.** Is each claim supported, or asserted and left hanging?

A draft that's weak on the first three is not "almost there." It needs a rewrite, not a polish. A draft
strong on those three and weak on the rest usually just needs another editing pass.

## Part 9: The failure modes that kill copy

These are the recurring ways copy goes dead. Each has a fix.

**The generic headline.** "Enterprise Solutions for Modern Teams" says nothing and could sit atop any page
in the category. Fix: add a specific benefit and a number.

**No differentiation.** "AI-powered, cutting-edge, enterprise-grade" is three claims every competitor also
makes, which means they're not claims at all. Fix: name the one specific thing you're best at and let go
of the rest.

**Company-centric copy.** "We built, we provide, we offer," which is more about the seller than the
reader. Fix: flip each sentence to what the reader gets.

**Zero specifics.** A page of "faster," "better," "more efficient" with no number anywhere. Fix: replace
each vague claim with an exact figure or a concrete example.

**Buzzword stacking.** "Leverage our synergistic platform to optimize and transform your workflow." Fix:
plain verbs. "Use" for "leverage." "Improve" for "optimize." "Change" for "transform." The plain word is
almost always the stronger word.

**The performed close.** A tidy, symmetrical, quotable final line that sounds profound and means little.
It's the sentence a writer is proudest of and the one a reader trusts least. Fix: state your point plainly
and stop. If the line survives being said out loud to a skeptical friend, keep it. If it only works on the
page, cut it.

## Quick reference

Run this on every piece before it ships:

- [ ] Knowledgeable-friend voice, not salesperson
- [ ] Addressed to one person ("you")
- [ ] Specific numbers, not adjectives
- [ ] Cut a fifth of the words
- [ ] Scannable structure
- [ ] Benefit before feature
- [ ] Every claim has proof
- [ ] One concrete offer, one CTA
- [ ] Specific headline (or everything below it is wasted)
- [ ] No performed closing line

If any box is empty, revise before you publish.
