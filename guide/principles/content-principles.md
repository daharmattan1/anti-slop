# Content Principles

A working reference for writing that reads as human. Each principle is a rule, a reason, and a concrete
before-and-after. They are not laws. They pull against each other on purpose (see `productive-tensions.md`),
and the craft is knowing which one wins in the moment. Read them once end to end, then keep them nearby
as a checklist.

---

## Voice and address

### 1. Write to one reader, not a crowd
Address the individual with the problem, not the abstract group. "If you've ever shipped a draft you
knew was thin, you know the feeling" lands. "Teams face growing content challenges" does not. The reader
cares more when they see themselves in the sentence.

### 2. Comfortable authority, not selling
You can mention what you know and speak in the first person, as long as it adds context instead of a
pitch. The test: remove the self-reference. If the sentence still gives the reader something, keep it. If
it only exists to promote you, cut it or reframe it. When you do have something to sell, name it plainly
and mark it as a plug. Hidden positioning erodes trust faster than an honest one.

### 3. Cut the jargon, keep the human
Use plain language even for technical subjects. Corporate abstractions ("leverage," "paradigm,"
"synergy") add nothing a concrete verb wouldn't. Conversational asides and a little personality are
allowed and welcome. The reader is a person; write like one is talking to them.

### 4. Earn authority through real experience
Strategic takes carry weight when they're grounded in something you actually did or saw. "After a few
years of watching this fail the same way" beats a confident assertion from nowhere. Deploy it when you
move from reporting facts to offering a judgment.

### 5. Observe, don't prescribe
Present the facts and the pattern; let the reader reach the conclusion. "The gap between launch and
copycat keeps shrinking" respects them. "You must act now or fall behind" talks down to them. Prescription
belongs in a sales page, not in writing meant to inform. Shift "you must" to "the data shows," and "is
now" to "is becoming."

## Concision and trust

### 6. Every word earns its place
Tighten hard. State the fact, give the take, stop. If the reader already understands the implication,
don't spell it out. Watch for the phrases that announce padding: "The implications extend beyond," "This
represents," "It's worth noting that." When you catch one, the fix is almost always to delete the whole
clause.

### 7. Trust the reader to subtract
When the facts make the point, adding "and this is significant" weakens it. "Announced in March. Copied
by April." needs no commentary; the two dates are the argument. Stating "this is fast" after showing the
timeline is like explaining a joke.

### 8. Cause and effect, not abstract framing
Explain relationships with a plain chain, not a mystified noun. "There's more work, the tool does it
faster, so the bottleneck moved" beats "this exposes the fundamental tension of modern work." Words like
paradox, collision, and tension are usually a signal that you skipped the actual mechanism.

### 9. Concrete specifics over vague strategy
When you name what matters, name the actual criteria. "What you weigh when you pick a tool: setup time,
who else is already on it, whether you can leave" beats "various workflows and requirements." Specifics
prove you know the terrain.

### 10. Active voice
Passive constructions add words and drain force. "Attackers weaponize the flaw" beats "the flaw is being
weaponized." "AI speeds up production" beats "production is being accelerated by AI." Aim for sentences
of ten to fifteen words, vary the length for rhythm, and cut the reflexive fillers: just, still, very,
even, really.

## Evidence and honesty

### 11. Extract the value before the click
When you cite a source, pull the specific number, quote, or example into your own text. Don't make the
reader click to learn anything. "The survey of 300 leaders found 85% raised budgets, yet 56% still call
them too small" gives value on the page. "Research reveals budget challenges (link)" makes them work for
nothing. If deleting the link would leave an empty sentence, you didn't extract enough.

### 12. Follow praise with the real tradeoff
Never let a positive claim stand alone. Say what's genuinely good, then name the honest cost, in the same
breath. "The new approach is faster to set up. It also locks you into one vendor's format." The tradeoff
has to land immediately, not three paragraphs later.

### 13. Qualify honestly
When you credit a strength or a trend, use language that shows judgment rather than cheerleading: some,
may, suggests, early indications, in limited cases. Attribute to the specific thing you saw, not the whole
category.

### 14. Keep sources clean
When you cover someone else's research, cite only their data. Don't fold in your own numbers to "validate"
it — that quietly launders your claim into their credibility. If your figures show up inside a section
about an outside report, you've mixed sources. Link to the primary source; be useful, not a gate.

### 15. Blunt judgment over academic caveats
Flag a source's limits the way a practitioner would, not with a methodology footnote. "Does this report
help them sell more of their own services? Sure. Doesn't make it wrong." tells the reader what you
actually think. "(Self-reported data, N=230, treat as indicative)" protects you and informs no one.

### 16. Challenge what your reader assumes
If your audience leans one way, open with the evidence that cuts against it. A room full of optimists
should meet the strongest case for caution first. Readers trust a source that challenges them more than
one that flatters their priors.

## Structure and flow

### 17. Build for the scanner
Most readers skim before they read. Use short paragraphs, bullets for parallel items, and visual breaks.
Frame with prose, list the examples, synthesize with prose. The rhythm of setup, list, payoff carries a
busy reader through.

### 18. One idea per sentence, short paragraphs
Optimize for a phone screen. Keep paragraphs to three or four sentences. Give each sentence one clear
idea and a clean period, not a trailing clause that re-explains it. If a paragraph looks like a wall on a
narrow screen, break it.

### 19. Turn colon-lists into real lists
A colon followed by three or more parallel items should become bullets, not inline sentences packed
behind one another. "Consider setup, adoption, and exit" is fine as prose; three full parallel clauses
after a colon are a wall. If the items share grammar and rank, make them a list.

### 20. Concrete impact before the analysis
On anything news-driven, lead with what happened and who it hit, then move to what it means. "The update
shipped Tuesday. Two large sites went dark. That's what everyone saw. Underneath it is a quieter pattern"
earns the analysis. Opening on "there's an interesting paradox here" makes the reader wait for the point.

### 21. Frame the stakes before the news
Establish why something matters before you report what occurred. An event is evidence for a frame, not
the frame itself. "If this technology is going to hold, two things have to be true" gives the reader a
lens; "Company X launched Y this week" makes them supply their own.

### 22. Thread the facts, don't list them
Connect fact to fact so one leads to the next. "We generate fixes people actually trust, which lets teams
clear backlogs they couldn't touch before" reads as thinking. "We generate fixes. People trust them. Teams
clear backlogs." reads as a list. Transitions between sections should bridge the ideas, not just pivot.

### 23. One spine across the whole piece
If a piece has several sections, the opening should name the frame that connects them, so the reader feels
one argument building rather than parallel essays stapled together. A single spine reads like one mind
working through a problem. Disconnected sections read like a committee, which is exactly what a machine
tends to produce.

### 24. Make the takeaway promote, not summarize
A closing section should lift the sharpest insight out of the body, not restate the body in new words.
Read the paragraph right before your "bottom line." If the bottom line says the same thing again, either
delete it or promote the real insight to take its place. The worst version is an unlabeled recap
disguised as a transition; replace it with the pointer it was trying to be.

### 25. Close on curiosity, not a to-do list
End a deep piece with an open question or an honest uncertainty, not a homework assignment. "It'll be
worth watching how this shakes out" respects the reader. "Audit your strategy this quarter" is a vendor's
call to action wearing an essay's clothes.

### 26. Open with a calm observation, not a drumroll
Start like a person noticing something, not a trailer building suspense. "Pricing is quietly
re-sorting itself" beats "Something is stirring in the world of pricing." The theatrical question and the
ominous "something is happening" are both tells.

### 27. Hold the tension, skip the tidy synthesis
Some things don't resolve. Let competing truths stand. "Both are partly right" or "the consensus is X,
but it's not that simple" trusts the reader to sit with it. A neat bow that resolves the ambiguity for
them is usually a lie you told to feel finished.

## Freshness and repetition

### 28. Use a metaphor once, then retire it
A vivid image is memorable because it's scarce. The first use lands; the fourth turns it into a slogan.
After the first time, either switch images or just say the thing plainly. Search your draft for your
favorite phrase — if it appears more than twice, vary it.

### 29. Don't recycle the same statistic
A powerful number belongs in one place, cited, with its source. When the same idea comes back, describe
it qualitatively instead of repeating the figure. Repeating a stat three times reads as obsession and
quietly erodes its credibility.

### 30. Catch the section that's secretly an ad
If a whole paragraph could drop into a sales deck unchanged, it's a pitch, not writing. Signs: it
describes what your thing does instead of what the problem is, it uses your exact category language, it
ends on an implied "so you need this." Analyze why the problem exists without steering toward your fix.
Honest problem analysis builds trust; hidden selling spends it.

## The machine tells

### 31. Avoid the obvious AI tells
Two constructions signal machine drafting on sight. The first is the **em-dash used as a default
connector**, dropped into a sentence and then again a clause later, until the whole paragraph reads as
automated; hold to roughly one or two in a piece and prefer "like," "including," "from X to Y," or a plain
parenthesis. The second is the **colon-sentence** ("The result: generic writing. The insight: purpose
beats polish."), which is the same instinct in a different shape; just state the thing, or break it into
two sentences. If either shows up more than a couple of times, rewrite.

### 32. Hunt the tells a word-search misses
The hardest tells aren't banned words. They live in the sentences between and after your points, and a
draft can be clean on every flagged phrase and still read as a machine. Three to hunt by hand:
- **Structural narration.** Prose announcing where you are in your own argument. "Having covered the
  what, let's turn to the why." "All of which circles back to the core question." Cut it; let the next
  point land on its own.
- **Faux-profound landings.** A closing line whose only job is to sound deep. "That's the whole game."
  "The value didn't disappear; it moved." Default to deleting it. End on a fact or a real question.
- **Performed stance.** Announcing a feeling instead of having it. "The part that genuinely surprised
  me," "a caveat worth saying out loud," "they did the work more people should." State the caveat plainly.
  Credit a source by citing what it did, not by praising it.

### 33. Humor with a point
A joke should do work: sharpen a contrast, signal independence, make a real argument land easier. Humor
for its own sake, or an inside joke the reader can't follow, is just noise with a punchline attached.

### 34. Connect to the live conversation
Ground the piece in what's actually being argued right now, not timeless pontification. "This is the same
fight people are having about remote work" or "we said something similar last month" gives the writing a
place to stand. Treating every piece as a standalone from nowhere is a machine's default, and it shows.

---

## The one-minute checklist

- Speaking to one reader, in plain language, without selling?
- Every word earning its place, no over-explaining?
- Specifics and real experience instead of vague strategy?
- Sources extracted, not just linked, and kept clean?
- Scannable, mobile-friendly, one idea per sentence?
- Metaphors and stats used once, not recycled?
- Em-dashes and colon-sentences under control?
- Structural narration, faux-profound landings, and performed stance hunted by hand?
- Ends on a real question or fact, not a homework assignment or a tidy bow?
