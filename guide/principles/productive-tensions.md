# Productive Tensions

The principles in `content-principles.md` don't all agree. That's the point. Good writing holds competing
rules in tension instead of mechanically obeying one, and a draft that fully satisfies a single rule
usually violates another. Treat each pair below as a dial, not a choice. Slide it based on the reader, the
format, and the moment.

## 1. Specificity vs. flow
One instinct wants every sentence to carry a fact, a number, a name. The other wants every sentence to
sound like a person talking. Pushed alone, the first reads like a spec sheet and the second says nothing.
**The dial:** lead with the human voice, then embed the specifics inside it. A reader tired of dry data
needs the warmth; a reader tired of fluff needs the anchor. Give them both in the same sentence when you
can.

## 2. Their words vs. your promise
Mirror the reader's own language and they feel understood. Paint where they could end up and they feel
pulled forward. Do only the first and you've described their present with no reason to move. Do only the
second and you sound like a brochure they don't recognize themselves in. **The dial:** open in their
words, close on your promise. Recognition first, aspiration second.

## 3. Density vs. the one remarkable thing
You can pack a piece with evidence until the case is overwhelming, or you can find the single striking
detail and build around it. More facts convince; one remarkable fact gets remembered and repeated. **The
dial:** if the piece isn't worth talking about, more facts won't save it, so find the one thing worth
talking about first, then support it. One vivid data point earns more attention than ten ordinary ones.

## 4. Simple vs. targeted
Plain language lets anyone follow you. Precise language makes exactly the right reader feel it was written
for them. Over-simplify and you lose the person who needed the specifics; over-target and you lose
everyone else at the door. **The dial:** simple words, targeted message. Don't reach for jargon to prove
expertise; reach for specificity to prove relevance. Lean simple at the top of the funnel, targeted near
the decision.

## 5. Confidence vs. honesty
A stance makes writing worth reading. An honest hedge makes it worth trusting. All confidence and you
overclaim; all hedging and you say nothing anyone can hold you to. **The dial:** state what you believe
plainly, and mark the edge of what you actually know. "I think X, and I'm not sure about Y" is stronger
than either half alone. The reader can tell the difference between a hedge that's honest and one that's
just fear.

## 6. Research vs. speed
One discipline says never write until you've done the homework. The other says write it fast, hear the
rhythm, then fix it. Research without drafting never ships; drafting without research ships confident and
wrong. **The dial:** three passes. Research first so you know the facts, draft fast so you find the voice,
then revise against the research so nothing false survives.
