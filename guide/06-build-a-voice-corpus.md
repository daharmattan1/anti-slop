# Build a voice corpus

A voice corpus is a small folder of passages you actually wrote, kept somewhere a drafting pass can reach them and continue from them. That is the whole object. Everything else in this essay is about what to put in it and why.

You need one because imitation-first drafting has a prerequisite: something of your own to imitate. The geometry essay covers why the alternative fails, why asking a model to write in your voice just averages everyone who writes a little like you and lands back at the generic center. The fix is not a better instruction. It is raw material, handed over in place of the instruction. And you have to build your own, because a borrowed corpus teaches the model to sound like whoever wrote it. The value is entirely in the samples being yours. There is no shortcut around collecting them, and you would not want one, since the collecting is where you start noticing what your voice actually does.

## Registers, as an idea

You do not write in one voice. You write in a few. There is the punchy one for a short post, the reflective one you reach for with room to think out loud, the plain teaching one you use to explain something you know to someone learning it. These are registers, and they differ enough that a sample from one drags a draft in the wrong direction when you use it for another. A reflective seed handed to a punchy post makes it long and slow.

So tag every sample with its register. You do not need a grand taxonomy. Name three or four registers you can actually feel the difference between, and file each passage under the one it belongs to. When you draft, pull the seeds that match the register you are writing in. Tagging is what turns the corpus into something usable rather than a pile.

## Signature passages, kept rough

The passages worth keeping are the ones that could only be you: a specific scene, a number you drop and walk past, a flat line where someone else would have performed. Those carry the fingerprint. The generic sentences in your own writing are not worth saving, because they point back at the same center you are trying to get away from.

Keep them exactly as you wrote them, typos and all. The instinct is to clean a passage up before filing it, and you should resist it. A sanded-smooth seed teaches sanded-smooth voice, which is a step back toward generic. The small roughness, the odd word choice, the sentence that does not quite resolve, is the part a drafting pass most needs to see.

The pass-order essay covers why a subtractive gate runs first without eating your signature lines. If a gate ever does flag one of these, mark it sacred, which the next section covers, and every automated pass will leave it alone.

## Capture your edits

The highest-value entries come for free, and most people throw them away. Every time you take a clean machine draft and rewrite it by hand into something that sounds like you, that edit is a labeled pair: clean-generic on one side, you on the other. The distance between the two is exactly what a drafting pass is trying to learn.

A real one looks like this. Before: "This approach offers several key benefits for teams looking to scale." After: "Three things get easier when the team grows, and one gets much harder." Same intended content, and the second version has a person in it who has actually watched a team grow. Add one line about what the edit changed, something like "cut the empty benefit-claim; name the specific things and admit the cost," and the pair is done.

So save the diff. When you rewrite a draft, keep both versions and the one-line note. Do not ship the good version and forget the bad one, because the bad one is half the lesson. A corpus of before-and-after pairs teaches more than a corpus of finished passages alone, since it shows the direction of the correction and not only where it ended up.

## Sacred spans

Some lines must never be touched by any automated pass. An exact quote. A sentence you fought for and got right. Words where the precise phrasing is the entire point. If a cleanup pass paraphrases one of those, it has broken something it cannot see it broke.

So mark them. Wrap the exact words in a marker your passes recognize and skip, and treat anything inside as immutable, edited around but never within. The marker travels with the text, so the line stays exact no matter how many passes the draft goes through.

## A corpus you can start today

Here is a template you can copy into a file and start filling. The registers are placeholders; replace them with your own.

```markdown
# My Voice Corpus
<!-- Raw material for imitation-first drafting. Grow it every time you approve a piece. -->

## Registers (name your own; these are examples to replace)
- R1 (Punchy / Short): how I write a quick post or a fast reply
- R2 (Reflective / Long-form): how I write when I have room to think
- R3 (Teaching / How-to): how I explain something I know to a beginner

## Signature passages (verbatim; keep your typos)
### Seed 001
- register: R2
- source: <post, email, draft, wherever it came from>
- date: YYYY-MM-DD
- passage:
> <2-3 sentences that could only be you>

### Seed 002
- register: R1
- source:
- date:
- passage:
> <...>

## Edit pairs (save these every time you hand-rewrite a machine draft)
### Pair 001
- register:
- date:
- before (the clean-generic version):
> <...>
- after (your hand-edit):
> <...>
- what the edit teaches (one line):
> <...>

## Sacred spans (never auto-edit; exact words only)
- id: <a short handle>
  text: "<the exact line no pass may change>"
```

Fill three seeds before anything else. Any three passages that sound like you, tagged and pasted verbatim, is enough to draft against, and the corpus compounds from there. Every piece you approve is another example of the real hand, so the more you keep, the closer each new draft starts to you instead of to the crowd. Start today with three passages, and add one more every time you write something you would put your name on.
