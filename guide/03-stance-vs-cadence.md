# A regex sees cadence. It can't see stance.

Here is a sentence that passes every mechanical check I own:

> The move most teams miss is how these tools actually relate to the work.

It has no banned phrases, no em-dash, clean grammar, ordinary length, and a perfect score on any lexical scanner. It is also unmistakably machine-made. You have read a thousand sentences shaped exactly like it and never once remembered one.

The tell was never in the tokens. A gate that counts em-dashes and matches banned phrases is reading cadence, the surface rhythm and texture of a sentence. It cannot read stance, which is the thing underneath: whether a person actually decided something and is willing to pay for having decided it. You can scrub a sentence spotless and leave it completely stanceless, and stanceless is the real smell of machine writing. The checker passed that sentence because there was nothing on its list to catch, and it could not tell that no one was behind it.

Look at what the sentence is doing. It flatters you, because you are about to learn the thing most teams miss. It claims an insight without committing to one, since it never says what the move actually is; it only performs the having of it. And it costs nothing, because nobody can disagree with a sentence that has not said anything a person could be wrong about. Three moves, and not one of them is visible to a scanner counting tokens.

Now the same idea with a stance in it:

> Most teams treat these tools like a faster autocomplete. I think that is a mistake, and an expensive one, because you get speed on the work you already knew how to do and nothing on the work you didn't. I would rather run a slower tool that changes what I am able to attempt at all.

Everything that changed is underneath the cadence. There is a real claim now, that treating the tools as autocomplete is a mistake, and a claim like that can be wrong, which means a reader can argue with it. There is a cost named out loud, the tradeoff between speed on the known work and nothing on the unknown. And there is a stated preference, a choice between two things the writer weighed before picking one.

The reason this matters is that the stance is the part that survives a rewrite. Take one position and say it two completely different ways, holding the grammatical person fixed so the only variable left is rhythm:

> Terse: I don't buy the autocomplete frame. It speeds the work I already knew how to do and moves nothing on the work I didn't, and I would rather run a slower tool that changes what I can attempt at all.

> Flowing: What makes me distrust the autocomplete frame is that it only ever pays off on the half of the job I had already figured out, and it leaves the other half, the part that would actually change what I am able to attempt, sitting exactly where it sat, which is the whole reason I would rather run a slower tool that moved it.

Both are first person and take the identical position, so the only thing separating them is rhythm. One is short and blunt, the other runs long and folds back on itself. Each carries the same claim, the same cost, and the same stated preference, only at a different tempo. You can change the cadence freely and the claim, the cost, and the preference all stay put. Rhythm is not where the stance lives, which is the whole reason a rhythm edit can neither install a stance nor remove one.

So keep the lexical gate. It is necessary and genuinely cheap, and it catches the obvious tells fast and for free. Just do not mistake it for a check on voice. It reads the surface. A sentence with zero violations has cleared the cheap checks and told you nothing about whether anyone is standing behind it.

The one thing a checker cannot do is want something. Stance comes from a writer who took a position and will be wrong about it in public, and there is nothing you can subtract from a clean sentence to manufacture that. So when a draft reads generic and still passes every rule, do not go hunting for one more rule. Put the harder questions to the paragraph in front of you. What does it actually claim? What would it cost you to be wrong? What did you choose? When the honest answer to all three is nothing, you were never looking at a cadence problem.
